## 3. Unauthorized practice of law (Oregon)

3.1 ORS 9.160(1), current text. **[live HTTP]** https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors009.html: "Except as provided in this section, a person may not practice law in this state, or represent that the person is qualified to practice law in this state, unless the person is an active licensee of the Oregon State Bar."

3.2 OSB UPL page. **[live HTTP]** https://www.osbar.org/UPL: a person who is not an active member but "drafts or selects legal documents" for others "may be engaged in the Unlawful Practice of Law."

3.3 OSB UPL FAQ. **[live HTTP]** https://www.osbar.org/UPL/faq.html: activities "generally not considered the unlawful practice of law in Oregon" include "sale of generic do-it-yourself legal publications without any further personalized assistance in preparation of documents or court papers." The same FAQ states the list "is not intended to be exhaustive" and that "it is not necessary that money change hands in order for conduct to be the practice of law."

3.4 Oregon Supreme Court and Court of Appeals decisions (the Gilchrist line).

- Oregon State Bar v. Gilchrist, 272 Or 552, 538 P2d 913 (1975). The Oregon Supreme Court held that advertising and selling do-it-yourself divorce kits with forms and completion instructions is not the practice of law, but that "all personal contact between defendants and their customers in the nature of consultation, explanation, recommendation or advice or other assistance in selecting particular forms, in filling out any part of the forms, or suggesting or advising how the forms should be used in solving particular customer's marital problems does constitute the practice of law" (272 Or at 563-64). Retrieval note: the passage is quoted verbatim and identically in Oregon State Bar v. Taub (below, full text read) and in OSB Formal Opinion No 2005-137 (below, full text read). This is an indirect quotation: not read from the decision's own full text. Direct full-text retrieval from two third-party reprint sources (FindLaw, OpenJurist) was blocked in this environment; a reprint is indexed at CourtListener (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1163543/oregon-state-bar-v-gilchrist/), which confirms the official citations and filing date (1975-08-07). CourtListener and FindLaw are third-party reprints of the official reporter text, not court-hosted sources.
- State Bar v. Security Escrows, Inc., 233 Or 80, 377 P2d 334 (1962), as quoted in Taub: "If the customer does not know what forms to use or how to direct their completion, then he needs legal advice. If the customer does know what he wants and how he wants it done, he needs only a scrivener."
- Oregon State Bar v. Fowler, 278 Or 169, 563 P2d 674 (1977), summarized in Taub: no violation where the nonlawyer used forms the customer supplied and the customer dictated the precise terms; no independent judgment exercised.
- Oregon State Bar v. Smith, 149 Or App 171, 942 P2d 793, rev den 326 Or 62 (1997), as quoted in Taub: the practice of law is "the exercise of professional judgment in applying legal principles to address another person's individualized needs through analysis, advice, or other assistance."
- Oregon State Bar v. Ortiz, 77 Or App 532, 713 P2d 1068 (1986), summarized in Taub: advising on the consequences of completing and filing forms requires legal skill and judgment.
- In re Morin, 319 Or 547, 878 P2d 393 (1994), summarized in Taub: a paralegal practiced law by exercising discretion in recommending which trust or will forms clients should use.
- Oregon State Bar v. Taub, 190 Or App 280, 78 P3d 114 (2003). **[live HTTP]** Full text read via FindLaw, a third-party reprint of the official reporter text, not a court-hosted source: https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/or-court-of-appeals/1429288.html. The Court of Appeals applied the Gilchrist line to a document-preparation service and held that a factfinder could infer the practice of law where the preparer may have exercised "independent judgment or discretion in the selection or recommendation of the forms provided to customers, selected the precise terms and information included in the documents, or provided advice regarding the customer's individual problems or the legal consequences of filing particular documents."

3.5 OSB Formal Ethics Opinion No 2005-137, "Unauthorized Practice of Law: Joint Venture to Produce Interactive Legal Information System" (approved by the Board of Governors August 2005; 2016 revision). **[live HTTP]** Full text: https://www.osbar.org/_docs/ethics/2005-137.pdf. Facts: a lawyer proposes a for-profit joint venture with a nonlawyer to offer a web-based legal information system for a fee; customers are told they will not communicate with or receive information from an individual; the system asks previously stored questions and provides previously stored legal information or forms based on the answers. Question: would the lawyer be promoting the unauthorized practice of law? Conclusion: No. Key passage: "The sale by nonlawyers of self-help legal software, whether through a program to be run on the purchaser's own computer or through a program to be run online, simply is not the practice of law, unauthorized or otherwise. When coupled with a clear indication to customers that there is no human interaction to be had, the absence of human interaction between a person seeking legal information, or advice on the one hand and a person providing that advice, is dispositive." The opinion reasons from Oregon RPC 5.5(a) and Gilchrist. Current status: listed in the OSB Formal Ethics Opinion Library table of contents at https://www.osbar.org/ethics/toc.html (accessed 2026-10-24) with no revision or withdrawal marker; the published text is the 2016 revision. Authority note: a formal ethics opinion is issued by the OSB Board of Governors and is advisory; it is not a court decision and binds no court. The OSB is the body authorized to investigate alleged UPL and to seek injunctions (ORS 9.164, 9.166); this records who issued the opinion, not any guarantee of enforcement outcomes.

3.6 Bounded negative findings. **[live HTTP]** The OSB's UPL advisory opinions page (https://www.osbar.org/UPL/advisoryopinions.html) lists three opinions (2013-01, 2013-02, 2014-03), none addressing document assembly; that page is a different series from the formal ethics opinions (3.5). No Oregon appellate decision addressing software- or website-based document assembly was identified in this review; this records what was not found on 2026-10-24 and is not an assertion that none exists.

3.7 How the project's design maps to this authority. [static source, 2.8 and 2.9] In-product, the tool presents previously stored questions and stored information and prepares previously stored official forms based on answers, with no human interaction - the pattern Formal Opinion 2005-137 describes. The Gilchrist/Taub danger zone is individualized selection, recommendation, and advice; the tool prepares one statewide packet for the user's chosen goal, drafts no alternative documents, gives no consequence advice, and stops to official help on uncovered facts. Whether these design choices keep the tool within the authorities above is a legal judgment the project does not make about itself. Nothing in this section restricts or conditions use of the tool; it records legal authority and review status. The out-of-product GitHub accessibility feedback channel is the one place a user could reach a human; the project has no documented policy for legal questions arriving there.

3.8 Review status. No completed qualified legal review is documented in the project materials reviewed (source tree at commit 62fdbe6, deployed site, public repository). This review cannot establish that no lawyer has ever looked at the project; it establishes only that no such review is documented. The source README and docs/legal-currentness.md contain inherited wording that material changes "require qualified legal review before release"; this records that the wording exists in the project materials, not that the owner has adopted it as policy, and the revised README does not carry it forward. Qualified legal review before future material changes remains a recommendation, not a rule.
